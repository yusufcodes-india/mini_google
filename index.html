💡 Why Are These Results Different?

Key Takeaway

1997 Search Engines (AltaVista, Lycos, Yahoo)

In 1997, search engines ranked pages primarily by what the page said about itself. They relied on word frequency, title tags, metadata, and exact phrase occurrences.

  • ❌ Vulnerable to keyword stuffing: A spammer could repeat "best camera" 20 times in white text on a white background to snatch the #1 ranking.
  • ❌ No trust verification: The engine had no way of knowing whether real humans trusted or valued the page.
  • ❌ Manipulable metadata: Commercial webmasters gamed meta keywords and title repeats effortlessly.

1998 Google (Larry Page & Sergey Brin)

In 1998, Google revolutionized the web by ranking pages according to what other pages said about them through hyperlinks and anchor text.

  • ✅ Links as democratic votes: Every inbound link is a citation endorsing the destination page.
  • ✅ Recursive prestige: Links from authoritative pages (like Stanford University or recognized testing labs) carry far more weight than links from obscure blogs or spam rings.
  • ✅ Anchor text context: Clickable link text (e.g., "recommended best camera") provides unbiased third-party descriptions of the target page.

Algorithmic Breakdown: 1997 vs 1998

1. 1997 Text Relevance Algorithm (TF-IDF & Keyword Matching)

Early search engines used Information Retrieval (IR) models inspired by library catalogs. The engine computes a score purely from document text tokens:

Score1997 = (ExactPhraseBonus) + (wtitle · TitleMatches) + (wtags · TagMatches) + (wurl · URLMatches) + (wbody · TermFreq)

Because the algorithm does not inspect backlinks, any page that maximizes term frequency naturally dominates the top results. This led to rampant "keyword stuffing" across the early web.

2. 1998 Google PageRank Algorithm

Developed at Stanford by Larry Page and Sergey Brin, PageRank models a "Random Web Surfer" who navigates links at random, occasionally jumping to an arbitrary page with probability (1 - d).

PR(A) = (1 - d) + d · ∑Ti → A [ PR(Ti) / C(Ti) ]
  • A: The target web page.
  • Ti: Any page with an outbound hyperlink pointing to A.
  • C(Ti): The total number of outbound links leaving page Ti.
  • d: The damping factor (default 0.85).
  • Dead-End Handling: Pages with zero outbound links have their PageRank distributed equally across all pages in the corpus to avoid absorbing all probability mass.

3. 1998 Google Composite Final Score

Google did not rank by PageRank alone—it combined on-page textual relevance, link graph authority, and anchor-text intelligence:

FinalScore = (0.55 · NormalizedTextScore) + (0.35 · NormalizedPageRank) + (0.10 · NormalizedAnchorScore)

This hybrid approach guarantees that a page must be both topically relevant to the search query and vouched for by the broader web ecosystem.
